## How the course is organized

The learning module consists of an introduction followed by **ten core
topics**. The topics build from tools and reproducibility through data
management and preparation to data analysis.

For each topic, the learning material has two complementary forms:

- a short, general `.pdf` presentation that introduces the topic; and
- more detailed `.md` guideline pages for self-paced study and application.

The presentations provide orientation. The guideline pages explain the
motivation, concepts, procedures, limitations, and further resources in more
detail. Use both forms: the slides are not intended to replace the guidelines.

The module also includes a continuous example project about **maize-yield
analysis**. It demonstrates how the topics can be connected in one data-science
workflow. The example is an application, not a restriction of the concepts:
the methods can also be transferred to laboratory studies, field experiments,
field observations, and other secondary-data projects.

## How the material is developed

The course material is under active development and is therefore subject to
change. Explanations, examples, links, source files, and generated documents
may be corrected or extended as the course is reviewed and learners provide
feedback.

The public repository contains the materials distributed to learners:

- [UKUDLA Introduction to Data Science public material](https://github.com/mmoessler/ukudla-intro-ds-public-material)

It includes:

- the current Markdown guideline pages;
- earlier committed states of those pages;
- the generated PDF presentations; and
- the LaTeX `.tex` source files used to build the presentation PDFs.

A generated PDF is convenient for reading, while its `.tex` source and commit
history make its construction and revision visible.

## Planned videos and office hours

The course team plans to add short videos based on the general presentation
slides. The purpose of the videos would be to guide learners through the main
motivation, concepts, and applications while the slides and guideline pages
remain the versioned reference material. The format, production process, and
publication schedule are still under consideration, so videos are not yet a
guaranteed component of every topic.

The role and format of weekly office hours are also still being developed. One
proposal is to use them to discuss questions, comments, and feedback submitted
to the topic discussions in the public GitHub repository. This would connect
asynchronous study with a recurring opportunity for clarification and shared
discussion. Any schedule and participation instructions will be announced
separately once they have been decided.

## How to follow changes

Each guideline page begins with three links:

| Link | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Source** | Open the current published Markdown source for that page |
| **History** | Inspect earlier versions and the commits that changed the page |
| **Feedback** | Open the discussion shared by all pages in that topic |

Before relying on a previously downloaded copy, check the Source or History
link. When a change matters for your work, record the version you used—for
example, with the repository commit identifier and access date.

---

## How to give feedback

The public repository provides one GitHub Discussion for each topic. Use the
Feedback link in any guideline page to reach the correct discussion.

Useful feedback is specific and actionable. Include:

- the page and section concerned;
- what was unclear, missing, incorrect, or especially helpful;
- what you expected to understand or accomplish; and
- a suggested correction or example, when possible.

Check whether the point has already been raised before starting a new thread.
Do not post passwords, access tokens, personal data, confidential research
data, unpublished results, or other sensitive information in a public
discussion.

---

## How to use the material for your notes

Use the publicly available material as the foundation for your own digital
notes. For example, you can:

- link from your notes to the relevant published page or section;
- write a short explanation in your own words;
- add commands, decisions, and examples from your own project;
- record questions to raise in the topic discussion; and
- note the commit identifier when your work depends on a particular version.

Keep a clear distinction between the published course material and your own
interpretation. A useful note records not only *what* to do, but also *why* it
is appropriate for your study design and data.
